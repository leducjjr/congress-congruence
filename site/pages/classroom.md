# For teachers

<p class="lede">Short activities for civics, government and statistics classes. The site is free,
needs no accounts or sign-ups, has no ads, and doesn't track students.</p>

## Before you start {#start}

- Students only need a browser. Everything works on phones and school laptops.
- Turn on **Hide party labels** (top of every page) to have students judge votes on their own
  merits first. You can share a link that opens with labels hidden: add `?parties=hidden` to any
  address, for example `uscongressvsvoters.org/states/?parties=hidden`.
- The [Glossary](glossary.html) explains terms like *roll-call vote*, *cloture* and *too close to call*.

## Activity 1: Meet your members (15 minutes) {#meet}

1. Open [All states](states/) and choose your state.
2. Find your two senators and your representative. (Not sure of your district? The House's
   [Find Your Representative](https://www.house.gov/representatives/find-your-representative) page
   looks it up from a ZIP code.)
3. For each member, write down: how many votes they missed this Congress, and how many surveyed
   votes went the way a clear majority of their constituents wanted.

**Discuss:** Is attendance a fair way to judge a member? What might explain a missed vote?

## Activity 2: Find a vote that went against the majority (20 minutes) {#against}

1. Open one of your members' pages and scroll to *Votes compared with what constituents said*.
2. Find a vote marked **Against the majority**. Open its official record.
3. Look up what the member said about that vote (a press release, a speech or a news story).

**Discuss:** Should a representative follow what most constituents want, or their own judgment?
This is an old debate: compare the "delegate" and "trustee" views of representation.

## Activity 3: Two senators, one state (20 minutes) {#compare}

1. Open [Compare](compare.html) and pick your state's two senators.
2. How often did they vote the same way? Find a vote where they split.

**Discuss:** Two senators represent exactly the same people. Why might they still vote differently?

## Activity 4: Reading an estimate (statistics, 25 minutes) {#estimate}

1. Open [Surveyed votes](surveyed.html) and choose a question.
2. Each state and district has an estimate and a *likely range*. Find one that's marked
   **too close to call**.
3. Read [Methods](methods.html#estimates) on how the range is worked out.

**Discuss:** Why are district estimates less certain than state estimates? Why does the site refuse
to count a vote when the range crosses 50%?

## Activity 5: Related isn't the same (15 minutes) {#related}

1. On [Surveyed votes](surveyed.html), find a vote labeled **related**.
2. Compare the survey question with what the bill actually did.

**Discuss:** Why doesn't the site count related votes? What could go wrong if it did?

## Good to know {#notes}

- The site compares; it doesn't grade. "Voted against the majority" isn't the same as "did
  something wrong", and the [FAQ](faq.html#wrong) says so for students too.
- Every figure links to its source, and every correction is in the [corrections log](corrections.html).
- Feel free to copy, print and adapt this page. Ideas for activities, or something that didn't work in
  class? [Tell us](contact.html#feedback).
