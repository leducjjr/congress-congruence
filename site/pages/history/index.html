The historical record, 1789–1972

Every recorded vote in the House and Senate from the 1st Congress, which met in New York in 1789, to the 92nd, which ended in January 1973: who served, how each of them voted, and how often they voted with their party. For 1973 to today, see All votes and Members by the numbers.

An older record, kept separately. Parties are those of the time, from Federalists and Whigs on; states, territories and districts changed often; and each vote's description is the clerk's brief note. There are no surveys of public opinion for these years, so there's no comparison with what voters said. More about this record

About this record

Everything in this section comes from Voteview, a long-running research project at UCLA that has compiled every roll-call vote in the House and Senate since 1789 from the official records of Congress. Each vote here links to its page on Voteview.

  • Who was a member. Voteview records, for every vote, whether each person was a member at the time. Members who arrived or left partway through a Congress are counted only for the votes while they served.
  • Parties. Each member's party is the one Voteview gives for that Congress, by its name at the time. "With their party" means the same as elsewhere on this site: how often they voted the way most of their party's members did, on votes where the party's majority took a side. In the earliest Congresses parties were loose groupings, so this figure says less about them.
  • Seats. States are shown as they were then. Some states elected all their representatives statewide, and district numbers were redrawn after each census, so the same number can mean a different place from one decade to the next.
  • Descriptions. Many are short, and the older ones were typed in capitals; they're shown here in ordinary sentence case, with the original kept on Voteview.
  • Totals. Voteview publishes each vote's Yea and Nay totals from the same records as each member's vote, so they always agree. That shows nothing was lost in loading, but it isn't an independent check the way the House Clerk's and Senate's published totals are for recent votes.
  • No surveys or topics. Large surveys of public opinion by state and district only begin in the 2000s, and Congress.gov's subject areas for bills begin in 1973, so neither appears here.
